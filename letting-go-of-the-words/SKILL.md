# Letting Go of the Words

Make web content a useful conversation initiated by its reader. Help people find an answer, understand it, and act with confidence. Concision serves that goal; a shorter page that loses necessary information is worse.

This skill synthesizes all 15 chapters and five interludes of Janice (Ginny) Redish’s second edition. It is an operational interpretation, not a reproduction of the book. The references preserve detailed lessons without loading them all for a small edit. No scripts, external services, or copy of the ebook are needed to use it.

## Start with the actual task

Use the supplied content, factual sources, audience evidence, brand guidance, and requested deliverable. For a small rewrite, infer a reasonable reader and proceed. For a consequential unknown—eligibility, price, deadline, responsible party—ask a focused question or clearly mark the gap; never invent the answer. Separate audience hypotheses from observed research.

Establish, briefly and usually internally:

- **Purpose:** What should the reader be able to do? How does that support the organization’s goal?
- **Reader:** What do they know, call things, need, fear, and have time for? What access constraints matter?
- **Conversation:** Why are they here, what do they ask first, and what comes next?
- **Page role:** Orient, help choose a route, answer a question, complete a transaction, or support deeper study?

Do not turn every request into a strategy project. Honor the requested format, language, tone, length, and editing scope. Use an existing style guide before proposing new conventions. A copy-only task does not authorize changing policy, redesigning navigation, contacting reviewers, or publishing.

## Work from questions to usable content

1. **Plan the conversation before polishing the old prose.** List the reader’s questions in the order they need answers. Include information they must know even if they would not think to ask. For a substantial rewrite, temporarily set the old structure aside, then reuse its verified facts as source material.
2. **Give the page the right job.** A pathway page helps people choose; it should not demand article-length reading. A destination page delivers the answer and a relevant next step. Keep one coherent task together; separate unrelated conversations.
3. **Put the useful message first.** Give the conclusion, answer, or action before organizational history. Use a short signal, a brief useful summary, and optional detail—Redish’s “bite, snack, meal,” credited in the book to Leslie O’Flahavan. Do not hide eligibility, material costs, warnings, or required steps in optional layers.
4. **Make the structure visible.** Use headings that expose questions, conclusions, or actions. Read the heading outline alone: it should tell a coherent story and make specific answers findable. Use semantic headings in implementation, not merely bold text.
5. **Write the conversation clearly.** Address the reader directly where appropriate. Name who does what. Prefer concrete verbs and familiar words; explain necessary unfamiliar terms. Put conditions before the actions they constrain. Keep connected sentence parts together. Retain a passive construction when it usefully focuses on the result or avoids blame.
6. **Choose the right form.** Use bullets for choices, numbered steps for sequences, and tables for comparisons or condition–answer relationships. Keep steps, alternatives, and outcomes distinct. Supply the facts and context needed to make the next decision.
7. **Make routes trustworthy.** Label links with the destination or action. Keep the destination heading consistent with the promise made by the link. Distinguish same-page navigation from departures. Offer relevant next steps without forcing the reader into an upsell or registration detour.
8. **Check the result.** Compare against the source for lost facts, altered obligations, omitted exceptions, invented claims, and changed links. Review headings, first sentences, and links separately. Read for natural spoken rhythm. For rendered work, inspect narrow screens, text enlargement, and relevant accessible interactions. State what was actually verified.

## Deliver the requested result

For a rewrite, lead with finished copy. Add only material assumptions, unresolved factual questions, and a short rationale if useful. Do not expose a full internal checklist on every small request.

For a review, prioritize concrete findings: where the conversation breaks, what the reader cannot find or do, the proposed fix, and supporting evidence. Distinguish observed user behavior from expert judgment. For an audit, include an actionable content disposition and owner when known. For a research plan, provide realistic tasks and comprehension criteria, not invented participants or results.

## Keep the principles flexible

- Readers sometimes want depth. Preserve useful evidence, nuance, required terms, and connected procedures; do not cut to meet an arbitrary word count.
- Reader questions help plan any page; they do not require turning the site into one giant FAQ.
- Familiar noun labels can work. Conversational writing need not be jokey, informal, or written entirely in questions.
- Numbers in the book—headline lengths, sentence lengths, list sizes, scroll limits—are editing prompts, not pass/fail rules or guaranteed conversion improvements.
- Apply the reader’s language and reading direction. Do not transplant English grammar rules, pronoun patterns, or left alignment blindly into other languages.
- Modern implementation note: decorative images may need empty alt text; charts need usable text equivalents; show/hide content needs accessible controls and state. Do not inherit the book’s simplified implementation claims. The sources reference explains these distinctions.
