# Review and testing

Book basis: chapters 14–15 and interludes 3–5. Contents: editing; collaborative review; style guides; news; legal information; research and validation.

## Edit in separate passes

A first draft gets the ideas onto the page. Revision improves what readers can do with them. Use passes proportionate to the task:

1. **Purpose and content:** Does the page answer the right questions? Is anything necessary missing? Does it belong here?
2. **Organization:** Do the headings and opening sentences form a useful sequence? Can readers find one answer? Are prerequisites and conditions visible in time?
3. **Meaning and accuracy:** Compare facts, quantities, dates, scope, permissions, obligations, and exceptions with authoritative source material. Repeated claims on multiple sites may all derive from one mistaken source. A search ranking is not a credibility guarantee.
4. **Language and tone:** Read aloud or simulate a spoken read. Revise places that require rereading, have unclear referents, or interrupt the main thought. Preserve the intended voice and precise meaning.
5. **Links and presentation:** Check destinations, labels, dates, visible hierarchy, layout, and relevant interaction behavior. Distinguish what was inspected from what was assumed.
6. **Proofreading:** Check words, grammar, punctuation, and accidental omissions separately from argument and flow. Spellcheck cannot catch a valid word in the wrong place. Reading line by line in reverse order can help isolate mechanical errors.

Let a draft rest when the schedule permits; do not impose a waiting period on routine work. Stop when the requested standard is met and the deadline calls for delivery.

## Use reviews to resolve actual issues

Developmental review examines purpose, audience, content, organization, and message. Copy editing examines details. Technical, legal, and policy review establish accuracy and sufficiency in those domains. One reviewer may cover more than one role, but the questions differ.

For a collaborative project, establish roles, scope, expectations, and timing early. A small before/after sample can help reviewers understand the intended conversational style before a whole document arrives. Share partial drafts when they can prevent wasted effort.

A review request should identify the draft stage, intended reader and outcome, specific questions, and due date. Name unknown facts directly: “Who approves this request?” is more useful than “Please review.” Do not send requests or schedule meetings unless authorized.

Consider comments respectfully without accepting them mechanically. Resolve conflicting facts using evidence. If someone misunderstands the draft, investigate whether the prose creates that ambiguity. For competing style opinions, return to the reader’s task, agreed voice, and observed behavior. Track significant accepted and rejected changes with reasons when a review process needs that record.

Walk through realistic scenarios as the intended reader before a participant session. Identify where an answer, choice, reassurance, or next step is missing. This is an expert walkthrough, not research with actual users. Do not report imagined reactions as observations.

## Maintain a small, useful style guide

Begin with the organization’s existing guide or adopted general reference. Add local decisions only where writers need them: terminology, voice, capitalization, dates, acronyms, recurring labels, list conventions, channel differences, and examples.

Keep entries short, searchable, and easy to link to. Record a choice and show its application. Appoint an owner and appropriate sponsor for a shared guide. Let questions and real disagreements drive additions. Offer a feedback route and check whether writers can actually find answers. Avoid building an encyclopedic rulebook before anyone can use it.

For a one-off task, state a proposed convention instead of silently changing an organization’s style policy. Do not turn every editorial choice into a durable personal preference.

## Treat online news as lasting public information

A press release may reach specialists, customers, and the general public directly, long after publication. Readers may use it as a summary, fact sheet, or primary explanation without realizing it was written for journalists.

Use a clear headline and key finding first, short sections, informative headings, relevant visuals, and links to the full report or authoritative detail. Label contacts by role and organization rather than leaving a name that readers might mistake for the researcher. Include a complete, unambiguous publication date with the year. Preserve historical dates when editing archived material.

Plan how news appears in search and shared previews. Give a useful short summary and ensure the linked page fulfills it. Establish how old news is marked, maintained, archived, or connected to current guidance. Do not quietly rewrite an old announcement as a current promise.

## Make legal information understandable without changing policy

The book argues that clarity, accuracy, and sufficient coverage can support one another. Use clear questions or statements as headings, direct language, short sections, and explicit actors. Separate dense citations from the main explanation when possible without losing their connection. Explain necessary technical terms at the point of use.

Preserve actual obligations, permissions, exceptions, defined terms, jurisdiction, and scope. Remove archaic wording only when the meaning survives. Do not turn a writing improvement into an assertion of legal equivalence. Use the relevant source and qualified reviewer for substantive legal decisions when needed.

The book’s model privacy notice is a writing example, not a current compliant policy. Never copy its assertions about cookies, data collection, sharing, or children into a real service without establishing the facts. Do not invent reassurance such as “we never share your data.” Retain a dated change history or update date when appropriate to the actual document.

## Test finding, understanding, and acting

Usability testing observes representative people attempting realistic tasks. It can start with existing content, a rough draft, or a paper prototype. It does not require a finished product, a lab, special software, recordings, or a lengthy report.

Plan around concrete uncertainties, choose participants relevant to them, give realistic tasks, observe behavior, interpret findings, and use the results. Include relevant disabled users, language groups, devices, and contexts instead of treating one convenient group as universal.

Tell participants they are trying the content; problems belong to the product rather than the person. Use neutral prompts. Do not lead them by putting the answer or the page’s exact label into the task. Avoid coaching unless the session design calls for it, and record assistance when it occurs.

Do not stop when a participant reaches the right page. Ask them to explain the answer in their own words or show the next action. Where did they hesitate, reinterpret a term, miss a condition, or take an unsuitable route? Ask what a picture communicates, not just whether it is attractive.

A lightweight task record can capture:

| Field | What to record |
| --- | --- |
| Research question | What is uncertain about this content? |
| Scenario | A plausible trigger and goal in the participant’s terms |
| Success criterion | Correct answer, decision, or completed action |
| Observation | What the participant did or said, including assistance |
| Interpretation | The likely issue, explicitly separated from observation |
| Change and recheck | Proposed fix, priority, owner if known, and how to check it |

Use small iterative sessions when the objective is diagnostic improvement. The book’s example participant counts and session durations describe approaches, not sample-size guarantees. No claim that all important issues have been found follows from testing a handful of people.

### Choose the method for the uncertainty

- **Moderated observation:** useful for understanding behavior and probing comprehension without leading.
- **Remote testing:** expands access to participants and their real environments; account for connection and setup limitations.
- **Unmoderated testing:** can efficiently reveal paths and outcomes; the ability to probe misunderstanding depends on the tool and study design. The book’s claim that it loses think-aloud detail is not universal with modern tools.
- **International testing:** requires appropriate language, local context, facilitation, and interpretation; translated wording alone does not settle cultural fit.
- **Simultaneous individual sessions:** can save time when facilitators use a consistent plan. A discussion afterward adds reactions but should not overwrite individual observations.
- **A/B experiments:** compare predefined outcomes under real conditions. Control changes well enough to interpret what caused a difference. Conversion data alone does not explain why it happened or establish comprehension. Do not invent sample-size or significance claims.
- **Focus groups:** useful for attitudes and discussion; group influence and self-report mean they are not substitutes for observing individual use. A task-based discussion can combine the two if the individual task evidence remains distinct.

Report what happened, what remains uncertain, and which changes are justified. Use observed results to revise and test again. If no participant work occurred, deliver an expert review or a test plan and label it honestly.
