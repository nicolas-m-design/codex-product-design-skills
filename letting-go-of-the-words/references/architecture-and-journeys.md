# Architecture and journeys

Book basis: chapters 4–7 and interlude 2. Contents: page roles; information architecture; content size and format; layering; marketing moments.

## Give each page an appropriate role

### Homepages orient and start useful work

A homepage should help visitors recognize the organization, understand what it offers, judge whether it is credible, start major tasks, and choose a route. Identity, a clear value statement, recognizable labels, and concise descriptions usually do more than a long welcome or mission statement.

Prioritize primary tasks instead of giving every department equal prominence. Let visitors begin a common task on the homepage when that saves useful steps. Keep important task controls visible alongside large imagery. Avoid forcing a lead form or account creation before visitors understand the offering and are ready to proceed.

Make search easy to find and consistently placed when the site needs it. Use audience vocabulary in the visible copy and navigation. People can arrive directly on a deep page from search or a shared link; provide enough identity and context there too.

Mobile is a useful constraint for prioritization, not proof of a different audience or permission to remove essential functionality. Establish whether mobile priorities differ through evidence.

### Pathway pages help people select and continue

Treat a pathway, category, or gallery page as a useful table of contents. Visitors are looking for a route; they often skip explanatory paragraphs. Put clear choices first. Use brief descriptions when they distinguish similar choices or explain unfamiliar names.

Product images alone may not distinguish technical products. Supply the decision attributes people need before opening every product—such as capacity, compatibility, price, or dimensions when those are known and relevant. Fragments are fine for these details. Provide meaningful text for people who cannot use the pictures.

A link’s words should strongly suggest the information behind it. If a page requires a long explanation of how to choose, first improve the choices and labels. Order them deliberately: people often take the first plausible option. Do not exploit that tendency to steer them into an unsuitable choice.

There is no universal three-click limit. A clear, slightly longer path can beat a short, confusing one. Repeated backtracking and opening candidates merely to discover what they contain suggest weak labels, missing comparison information, or a poor structure. Treat these as hypotheses until observed.

### Destination pages complete a conversation

Confirm where the visitor has arrived, answer the main question early, and supply detail in the order it becomes useful. Offer a logical next step where there is one: complete the action, compare a related option, see evidence, get support, or return to the relevant category. Do not strand a ready-to-act reader after presenting useful facts.

## Organize around meaningful distinctions

Possible organizing principles include:

- Questions people actually ask.
- Topics or tasks they recognize.
- Product categories that match their mental model.
- Information types, such as policy, procedure, news, and evidence.
- Audience roles with clearly different needs.
- Life events or stages.
- Time, sequence, or process phase.

Avoid exposing the organization chart as the primary structure unless users genuinely need and understand it. Use card sorting or other audience evidence when grouping is uncertain; do not present an expert’s proposed structure as a validated one.

Audience-based navigation works only if people recognize the right group, accept its label, and can account for wearing different hats on different visits. Prefer task routes when role choices overlap or exclude people. Sometimes only one part of the site needs role-based branches.

Connect information laterally across categories: policy to procedure, announcement to report, advice to relevant products, and back where helpful. Coordinate editorial and marketing plans so informative content supports real next steps. A page’s ownership should not determine whether it can link to another team’s page.

## Choose the right size and delivery format

Think of reusable units of information rather than uploading the document that happens to exist. A useful unit might answer one question or a connected sequence of questions. Do not make readers open many tiny pages to finish one coherent procedure.

Split when audiences, decisions, or conversations are genuinely different. Keep together what people need to compare or follow as one task. Consider loading costs, interruption, mobile reading, accessible navigation, printing, and deep linking. If a useful page is long, offer an informative structure and suitable in-page navigation; do not enforce an arbitrary number of scrolls.

Use HTML for information people need to find, skim, and act on within the site. PDF can be appropriate for a report, archival document, print layout, or requested download. Sometimes both formats serve different needs. Do not turn an explicit PDF assignment into an unsolicited HTML project.

For necessary downloads, identify the format and useful size information, give a clear summary or access route, and ensure the document itself is usable. Searchable text alone does not make a PDF accessible. Preserve reading order, headings, alternatives for images, and table relationships. Provide printable versions that do not clip text or force people to print unrelated material.

## Put answers first and detail within reach

Use layered depth when readers need different amounts:

- **Bite:** a useful headline or link, possibly with a short description.
- **Snack:** the conclusion or summary sufficient for a quick answer.
- **Meal:** evidence, explanations, or detailed instructions for people who need them.

These are functional roles, not three mandatory blocks on every page. A short answer may already be complete. A serious decision may require substantial detail. Readers should not have to navigate to the “meal” to discover a condition that invalidates the snack.

For substantial rewrites, outline the reader’s questions before reusing old paragraphs. Check remaining source material for necessary facts and unanticipated needs before discarding it. Replace organizational background with relevant context rather than deleting context indiscriminately. Reassurance can earn its words when it explains what will happen or resolves a real worry.

Use progressive disclosure for optional explanations without losing the current task. Label the extra information clearly. Do not hide required actions in collapsed content or depend on hovering. In implementation, use accessible controls, state, focus behavior, and reading order; merely adding JavaScript does not ensure accessibility.

## Find appropriate marketing moments

A visitor who arrives voluntarily brings a purpose. Start by meeting it. Relevant recommendations can then continue the conversation: related supplies after a product choice, a clear application action after the terms, or a genuinely useful next article.

Marketing works when it supports the current decision, not when it interrupts it with an unrelated pitch. Avoid hiding prices or support information behind self-praise. Conversely, do not provide attractive facts and leave no way to act on them.

Make links, assistance, and contact information available where people need them, not only in global navigation. Let the visitor decide when the conversation is complete. Social posts and previews can provide a useful bite leading directly to the matching answer; avoid misleading teasers and generic homepage destinations.
