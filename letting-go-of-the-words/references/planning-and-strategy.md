# Planning and strategy

Book basis: chapters 1–2 and interlude 1. Contents: outcomes; audience evidence; personas and scenarios; strategy and governance; inventory and audit.

## Define success through the reader’s success

People arrive to answer a question, make a decision, perform a task, learn, or enjoy something. Navigation and search are means to those ends. State the purpose as “This content helps [people] do [specific thing], supporting [organizational goal].” Replace vague objectives such as “inform” with an observable outcome.

Success includes finding, understanding, and using the information within the effort the reader is willing and able to spend. A fast exit can mean a perfectly answered question or immediate frustration; traffic and time-on-page do not establish which. An objective to reduce calls must not remove a needed contact route. Helping someone leave successfully may earn a return visit.

Treat every page, form, message, image, and downloadable document as part of a conversation. The whole site, each section, and each content item can have different but compatible purposes. Integrate accessibility and discoverability into those purposes from the outset.

## Learn the reader’s reality

Use available evidence before relying on intuition:

- Search terms reveal needs and vocabulary. Retain the actual terms rather than translating them into departmental jargon.
- Customer questions, support interactions, page feedback, reviews, and public discussion reveal recurring confusions and missing answers.
- Existing marketing and UX research can reveal useful audience differences; distinguish research from assumptions.
- Analytics show routes and outcomes but rarely explain motives. Pair them with observation or questions.
- Contextual observation shows how people work in their environment. Critical-incident interviews elicit specific episodes rather than hypothetical preferences.
- Short surveys can establish why someone arrived and whether they succeeded. Usability sessions reveal what happens when they actually use the content.

Use only evidence available within the task’s authorized scope. The book’s research suggestions are not instructions to access private channels, recruit participants, or send messages without authorization.

Group people by needs that affect content: expertise, task, relationship to the service, or stage of a journey. A department or building is not a person. Relevant differences include:

| Dimension | Content decision it informs |
| --- | --- |
| Language and exact phrases | Search vocabulary, labels, definitions |
| Expertise and experience | Prerequisites, depth, necessary terminology |
| Emotional state | Reassurance, directness, tone, tolerance for delay |
| Values and priorities | What information supports a decision |
| Device, bandwidth, assistive technology | Content form, load costs, usable presentation |
| Physical and social setting | Interruptions, privacy, one-handed use, finding one’s place again |
| Cultural and linguistic context | Ambiguous dates, idioms, images, translated concepts |
| Relevant demographic or ability differences | Actual access needs, avoiding stereotypes |

Experts can be novices outside their specialty. High literacy does not imply patience for convoluted prose. Age alone does not establish technology competence, attitude, or needs. A majority behavior does not erase the people outside it.

## Use personas and scenarios as reasoning aids

A persona is a research-grounded composite, not an invented customer passed off as evidence. Preserve the observations behind it. If no research exists, use a brief, clearly labeled audience hypothesis instead of an elaborate fictional biography.

Include only attributes that change decisions: goals, relevant knowledge, vocabulary, pressures, access constraints, and recurring questions. A respectful name, quote, or picture may help a team remember a persona; these are optional aids, not proof. Do not invent demographic precision or use a recognizable photo without appropriate permission.

Represent materially different needs, including disabled users, without pretending a single persona covers every access need. A homepage may serve several groups; a detailed page may serve one. Secondary audiences may need only lightweight scenarios.

Turn goals into situations: what triggered the visit, what the person knows, what they need, and what a successful end looks like. Trace the conversation from arrival through follow-up questions. Every content item should serve a plausible scenario or a documented obligation. If neither exists, question its place on the site.

## Make strategy maintainable

A content strategy connects organizational goals, reader needs, messages, channels, people, process, and technology. It covers text, forms, images, data, audio, video, downloadable material, and social content. Coordinate web, support, product, marketing, print, and email so contradictory answers do not emerge across channels.

Define the priority of messages, the organization’s recognizable voice, and deliberate differences in tone for specific situations or media. Channel adaptation can change the form without changing the facts or core message. Define who creates, edits, approves, publishes, maintains, and retires material. Give ongoing social activity realistic staffing and moderation plans rather than promises nobody can sustain.

Let actual content needs inform templates and CMS requirements. Design with representative content early, including awkward lengths and exceptional cases. Do not accept a template’s arbitrary field limit as a reason to omit a necessary answer.

## Inventory, audit, and act

Scale this work to the request. A full inventory is unnecessary for one paragraph.

1. **Inventory what exists.** Record URL or location, title, purpose, content type, inbound/outbound connections, dates, and current owner when known.
2. **Set the intended direction.** Agree on messages, audience needs, tone, formats, and usable content templates. Begin the local style guide with decisions already made.
3. **Audit quality and fit.** Check accuracy, freshness, duplication, missing answers, task alignment, findability, readability, and accessible delivery. Consult actual search, support, and usability evidence where available.
4. **Choose a disposition.** Keep, revise, combine, split, move, retire, or create. Explain the reason. A low-traffic page may still meet a critical need or obligation; do not retire it solely on traffic.
5. **Make the work executable.** Record priority, owner, dependencies, due date, and status if known. Identify missing information and ownership explicitly rather than inventing assignments.
6. **Check the new experience.** Use the resulting content map and real tasks to test whether readers can complete their conversations.
7. **Plan maintenance.** Establish review triggers or dates, an accountable owner, and what happens when information expires or changes. Creating a schedule or retiring live content requires the user’s task to authorize that action.

Inventory is a list of what exists; audit is an assessment; neither produces improvement until someone acts. Work across departmental boundaries to find related information, not just within the folder owned by one team.
