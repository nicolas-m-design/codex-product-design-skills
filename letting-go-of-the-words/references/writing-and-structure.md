# Writing and structure

Book basis: chapters 7–12. Contents: headlines; section headings; sentences and voice; lists; tables; links. Examples below are original teaching examples, not quotations from the book.

## Write headlines that work without the page

A headline may appear alone in search results, navigation, a shared preview, or a mobile list. It must identify the subject and attract the right readers without promising something the content does not provide.

Use recognizable vocabulary and enough specificity to distinguish this page. Prefer a clear answer, question, or action to an unexplained pun. Avoid local acronyms and idioms for a broader audience. If a short label does not carry enough meaning, add a descriptive phrase or a brief summary alongside it.

A familiar category label such as “Shoes” can work in navigation; an article titled “Options” usually needs more context. There is no universal optimal word count. Judge whether the headline is specific, truthful, scannable, and suited to where it appears.

In HTML, distinguish the page’s visible main heading from its document title. Coordinate them around the same subject and promise. Use semantic heading markup; font size alone does not provide structure. Do not promise rankings or copy old click-through statistics as guarantees.

## Build and test the heading outline

Headings make the organization visible. They help readers scan, find one answer, understand relationships, and resume after interruption. They also help the writer discover repetition or missing logic.

When revising, try naming the point of each existing section. Difficulty naming one often signals mixed or unclear content. Repeated headings often signal content that should be reorganized or combined. This is a diagnostic pass, not permission to insert arbitrary headings every few paragraphs.

Choose forms deliberately:

| Form | Best use | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reader’s question | A recognizable concern needing an answer | “Can I change my delivery address?” |
| Statement | A conclusion worth seeing while scanning | “Address changes close before dispatch” |
| Action phrase | A task or instruction | “Change your delivery address” |
| Familiar noun label | A category with a shared meaning | “Delivery options” |

Use the reader’s questions rather than disguising organizational self-description as an FAQ. A separate FAQ may indicate the main content failed to answer essential questions; improve the authoritative topic rather than maintaining inconsistent duplicates.

Keep questions focused. If a heading contains several conditions, see whether those belong in the answer or in separate sections. Give sections distinct opening words; repeating “How do I…” or the same keyword in every heading makes scanning slower. Where useful, combine a recognizable term with a short question, but avoid keyword stuffing.

Use parallel structures among comparable headings. Depart from the pattern when the role genuinely changes, such as an important aside. Maintain a logical, shallow hierarchy suited to the content; reorganize if it becomes unnecessarily deep, but do not flatten meaningful relationships to satisfy a numerical limit.

Make heading levels visually distinguishable and associate each heading with what follows. Use semantic levels in order. Avoid making noninteractive headings look like links. Sentence case is a useful default unless the established style says otherwise.

For long pages, a clearly identified “On this page” list can help readers jump to an answer. Distinguish it from navigation to other pages. Place necessary context where it will be seen even by a person who jumps directly to a section. The book’s preferred position is a heuristic; follow the product’s tested navigation patterns.

Read only the headings and ask:

- Can the intended reader predict what each section contains?
- Does the sequence follow the reader’s problem rather than the writer’s source document?
- Can someone locate one answer without reading everything?
- Does the outline reveal the gist without making unsupported claims?

## Tune sentences without losing meaning

### Keep the participants clear

Use “you” when speaking to the reader, “we” for a clearly identified organization, and direct verbs for instructions. Do not alternate between addressing the reader and discussing “the user” without a reason.

In reader-led Q&A, “How do I…?” pairs naturally with “You can…”. In a form, the site may ask “Do you…?” and the response may be “I…”. An author can use “I” for their own experience. The rule is clear speaker identity, not mandatory pronouns in every sentence.

Objects, research findings, and third-party situations may need ordinary nouns instead. Use inclusive references without awkward workarounds; follow a specific person’s pronouns. Do not reproduce the book’s dated binary framing of gender.

### Name actors and actions

Prefer a direct actor–verb–object structure when readers need to know responsibility. Changing “Approval is required” into “We approve…” is not safe if the source does not identify who approves. Find out or flag the gap.

Passive voice can be appropriate when the result is the focus, the actor is irrelevant, or naming the reader would assign blame unnecessarily. Do not mechanically remove all passive constructions or all uses of “is.”

Move actions out of abstract noun phrases: “Submit your application” is clearer than “Complete the submission of your application.” Keep the exact meaning of technical, contractual, or organizational terms when a simpler replacement would change it.

### Follow the reader’s logic

Put a condition before its action so people do not act before noticing the restriction. For example, “If the payment failed, try again” is safer than “Try again if the payment failed.” This takes precedence over starting every instruction with a verb.

Introduce known context before new detail. Keep the subject near its verb and the verb near its object. Move inserted dates, addresses, qualifications, and background into their own sentence or a useful labeled detail. Do not separate a crucial condition from the action it limits.

Give one main idea per sentence, or a few tightly connected ideas. The book’s short-sentence target is an editing aid, not a rigid quota. Very short sentences and fragments can work in cards, labels, and brief answers. Vary rhythm where it aids understanding rather than producing a choppy sequence.

### Cut what slows understanding

Remove repeated points, empty introductions, needless self-reference, and inflated wording. Retain necessary examples, evidence, uncertainty, exceptions, and reassurance. Compare the revision with the source for scope changes: “may” is not “will,” “usually” is not “always,” and an estimate is not a promise.

Keep paragraphs focused and short enough to scan. One sentence can be a paragraph; not every sentence must be one. Read the opening sentence of each paragraph by itself to test the flow.

Use words the audience knows. Specialist vocabulary can be exactly right for specialists. Explain unfamiliar necessary terms at the point of need. Avoid decorative jargon and colloquialisms that obscure meaning across cultures. Plain language can be precise, formal, serious, and distinctive; it does not require a chatty voice.

## Use lists for sets and sequences

Use bullets for options or unordered items. Use numbers for required order. Numbers can also signal a count or ranking, but do not mix that use with procedure steps in a way that implies every option must be completed.

For procedures:

- State prerequisites before the step that needs them.
- Separate actions so readers can do one and reliably find the next.
- Show expected results under the relevant step rather than making them another action.
- Make branches explicit. A small condition–action table under a decision step can be clearer than two numbered alternatives that look cumulative.
- Include a screenshot or diagram only where it resolves uncertainty.
- Try the sequence when the environment permits; do not claim it works solely because the prose is polished.

Keep comparable items parallel and front-load distinguishing information. Group long unfamiliar lists with useful headings. A familiar alphabetical list, such as regions or countries, can legitimately be long; “short lists” is not a reason to fragment it arbitrarily.

Formatting matters: keep the introduction visually connected to the list, align wrapped lines with the text rather than the bullet, and give long items breathing room. Distinctive bullet graphics should support the tone without suggesting nonexistent categories. In implementation use actual list semantics.

## Use tables to reveal relationships

A list contains one kind of item even if it flows into several visual columns. A table relates two or more dimensions. Use tables for comparisons, recurring condition–answer pairs, or repeated sentences whose shared wording can become headers.

Choose the first column from what the reader already knows and scans for: their situation, product, category, or relevant range. Put the corresponding answer beside it. For a decision table, put the condition before the consequence.

Keep units, ranges, currencies, and exceptions explicit. Preserve boundaries such as “under,” “up to,” and “at least”; do not introduce overlaps or gaps when converting prose to rows. Do not fill unknown cells with guessed values.

Keep tables simple enough to use on the target screen. If many independent variables control the answer, a guided series of questions, filtering, or several focused tables may work better. Keep comparison information together when readers need to compare it; split only when that helps the task.

Provide clear headers and accessible relationships. Keep headers available for long tables. Align text consistently with the reading direction and numbers by their comparable place value where possible. Use subtle rules or shading to help people follow a row; heavy boxes can compete with the data. Preserve relationships in a mobile presentation instead of turning columns into ambiguous unlabeled fragments.

## Make links tell the truth

An unfamiliar product or program name needs descriptive context. Use the destination or action as the link text; avoid a list of identical “More” or “Click here” links. “More about delivery times” is meaningful; “More” alone is not.

Keep the link promise and the destination heading recognizably aligned. Exact repetition often helps but is not mandatory; consistent keywords and meaning matter. Review labels as a set and in isolation, including the experience of a screen-reader link list.

For actions, use an explicit verb and object when that makes the result clearer. A familiar navigation noun does not automatically need “Go to” appended.

Consider the timing of a link. An optional departure in the middle of a necessary instruction can interrupt the task. Put related reading after the relevant explanation. Inline citations, definitions, and browsing-oriented articles can appropriately use embedded links; there is no blanket ban.

Ensure links look interactive. Preserve useful visited-state differentiation where it helps people avoid retracing routes. Include a linked icon or bullet in the same usable target as its label when implementing. Keep accessible names meaningful, avoid duplicate keyboard stops for the same destination, and check the actual destination rather than assuming a well-written label is sufficient.
